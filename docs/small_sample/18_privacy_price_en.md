# 18. Can bounding each person's contribution cut the price of privacy? (pre-registered test)

*Pre-registered: the plan `configs/selfsel_price_registry.json` (10 hypotheses P1–P10, thresholds, two named decisions) was committed in `d811aae` before the test cells of the final run were written (two smoke cells at 100 people were run earlier and carry no hypothesis; section 5, point 11). Verdicts are computed by `scripts/ssp/an_price.py`. Anything marked "post hoc" was not in the plan. Tables: `results/small_sample_popularity/03_tables/price/`.*

## 0. Summary

1. **All 10 registered hypotheses were met and both named decisions came out positive.** Bounding each person's contribution more tightly than the 10-row cap of doc 16 cuts the price of privacy a lot at 1,000 people and less at fewer people. Of the four bounds tested, unit-length reports (every person's report scaled to length 1) had the lowest mean price; that is registered only at 1,000 people and does not hold on the 3 Goodreads logs there. C = 1 is the tightest possible clip (any C ≤ 1 gives the same lists), not a tuned optimum.
2. **The selector's price with unit-length reports instead of the 10-row cap** (list quality lost against the same selector without noise, mean over logs; ε per round; central model, nominal accounting; only the 1,000-people entries at ε = 2, 4, 8 are registered, the rest is post hoc): at 1,000 people **0.273 → 0.082** at ε = 4 (−70%), 0.422 → 0.199 at ε = 2, 0.134 → 0.031 at ε = 8; at 300 people 0.446 → 0.261 at ε = 4 (−41%), 0.531 → 0.401 at ε = 2 (−24%), 0.329 → 0.128 at ε = 8 (−61%); at 100 people 0.484 → 0.384 at ε = 4 (−21%) and 0.410 → 0.274 at ε = 8 (−33%). In list-quality terms at ε = 4 (post hoc): 1,000 people reach 0.679 against 0.762 without privacy (−11%), 300 people 0.445 against 0.706 (−37%), 100 people 0.245 against 0.629 (−61%).
3. **Registered gains over the 10-row cap (selector value, 95% interval over logs):** +0.191 (+0.133 to +0.245) at 1,000 people and ε = 4 (P1); +0.185 (+0.142 to +0.224) at 300 people and ε = 4 (P2); +0.223 at ε = 2 (P3); +0.103 at ε = 8 (P4). Strong privacy still costs: 0.199 at ε = 2 and 1,000 people (P6, at least 0.10 expected); weak privacy is cheap: 0.031 at ε = 8 (P6, at most 0.06 expected).
4. **Much of the gain is tight bounding as such, more so with more people (post hoc).** A hard cap of one random row per person reaches 79–83% of the unit-length gain at 1,000 people, 65–77% at 300 and 39–63% at 100. Unit length adds a smaller part over a one-row cap, separately tested: +0.032 at ε = 4, +0.043 at ε = 2, +0.022 at ε = 8 (P9, P10); it also beats the looser bound C = 2 by +0.084 (P5). Without noise the bound costs unit length 0.008 (P8) against 0.027 for the one-row cap (post hoc). The split depends on the order of comparison, because a one-row cap carries more bias than unit length.
5. **The selector keeps working under unit-length reports** (P7): mean regret 0.0017; better than a pooled table by 0.0245 (−0.0476 to −0.0071).
6. **A caveat the registered tests hide (post hoc; three logs are too few for an interval, so these are descriptions).** At 1,000 people the gain comes from the 12 Amazon logs (+0.274, +0.239, +0.131 at ε = 2, 4, 8). On the 3 Goodreads logs, where people have many rows, unit length gives +0.018 at ε = 2, −0.003 at ε = 4 and −0.009 at ε = 8 against the 10-row cap, and the looser bound C = 2 does best there. At 300 people the 5 non-Amazon logs gain (+0.128, +0.074, +0.040 at ε = 2, 4, 8).
7. **A tight bound shortens the best memory at 1,000 people** (post hoc; medians on a doubling grid, no interval): 112 days with unit length against 896 days with the 10-row cap at ε = 4, 56 against 224 at ε = 8 (56 days without privacy). At 300 people and ε = 4 it is 896 days with either bound. The finding of doc 16 that noise lengthens the memory depends on how much noise the bound lets in.
8. **All prices are central-model prices with nominal accounting, not certified guarantees** (section 5, point 1). A trusted server sees every person's rows; ε is per round and adds up over repeated rounds (a person drawn often loses a multiple of it); a replace-one reading of "neighbouring" doubles the sensitivity, so a cell labelled ε is roughly 2ε in replace-one terms (my inference from σ ∝ sensitivity/ε) and the ε = 2 column is the nearest tested to a replace-one ε = 4 (0.199 at 1,000 people, 26% of the list quality); the number of people in a round is released without noise; the selector's self-score and arm choice are post-processing of the noisy release; C = 1 was chosen with non-private development data from earlier windows of the same logs.

## 1. What was done

1. **Why this test.** In doc 16 the selector lost 0.308 NDCG at ε = 4 per round and 1,000 people, and 0.051 at ε = 8 and 3,000 people, with the 10-row cap inherited from doc 12 (S9 and S11 not met). The Gaussian noise that makes a count private grows with the largest contribution one person may make, and the cap of 10 was never tuned although most person-weeks hold one or two rows (72% of person-weeks on the 20 Amazon logs have a single row; 50% on the 7 other logs). The literature read for doc 90 (section 6, item 5) says the same trade-off exists in general: limiting each person's contribution keeps the noise low and adds bias (Amin et al. 2019); weights that shrink a heavy person's rows smoothly can do better than a cut-off (Epasto et al. 2020); a hard cap per user "can add bias and significantly undermine the utility" and weighted normalisation is an alternative (Bagdasaryan et al. 2022).
2. **The bounds compared** (all with the central Gaussian mechanism of doc 16: analytic Gaussian, δ = 10⁻⁵, noise on every item already in the catalogue, ε per round):
   - **cap 10** — at most 10 random rows per person, sensitivity √10 (the doc 16 baseline);
   - **cap 1** — one random row per person, sensitivity 1;
   - **clip C = 2** — every person's count vector (item → rows in the round) is scaled to L2 norm at most 2, sensitivity 2;
   - **clip C = 1, "unit length"** — every person's vector is scaled to L2 norm 1 (a person with one row is unchanged; a person with r distinct rows gives each row the weight 1/√r), sensitivity 1;
   - each with a **noise-free reference** (ε = 10⁶), to separate the loss of the bound from the loss of the noise.
3. **The cells.** Window w2 (the confirmation windows), 7-day rounds, 100, 300 and 1,000 people (27, 21 and 15 logs; 63 cells), ε = 2, 4, 8 per round. The selector and the regret are those of docs 14–16. The **price** of a rule is its NDCG in the stored non-private plain-sampling cell minus its NDCG under the sampler. The plain-sampling arms of every cell reproduce the stored confirmation cell exactly; the analysis stops if they do not (checked: 63 of 63).
4. **Development (in-sample, window w1).** Four grids of 17, 24, 7 and 48 cells on the weekly-study windows chose the plan: at 1,000 people and ε = 4 the selector's price was 0.308 with cap 10, 0.199 with C = 2 and 0.095 with C = 1 (cap 1: 0.121); across nine (people, ε) combinations C = 1 beat C = 2 and C = 2 beat cap 10 every time; without noise the bound alone cost at most 0.006 (C = 1). Because every non-empty person has L2 norm at least 1, any C ≤ 1 scales every person to norm exactly C, and the noisy counts are then exactly C times those of C = 1 (σ is proportional to C), so no smaller bound was tried (derived and checked by the reviewer, not a separate experiment). These numbers are optimistic and are not results.
5. **Pre-registration.** Thresholds are about half of the development gains. A same-provider design critic (three passes), a code reviewer and two probers shaped the plan before the freeze: they asked for the hard-cap comparison (P9, P10), for a decision that does not credit unit length for the effect of any tight bound, for nominal-accounting caveats, for thresholds tied to the development numbers, and removed an Amazon-only hypothesis that nearly repeated P1.
6. **Run.** 63 cells under the quiet governor on 2026-10-16. Two things interrupted it (disclosed in section 5, points 8 and 9): a 4-hour stall caused by my own swap-space guard, and one worker I terminated by mistake while stopping another job, which rebuilt the worker pool and re-ran one cell. No cell failed and none was dropped.

## 3. Interpretation

1. **At 1,000 people, doc 16's price of privacy was largely the price of its 10-row cap.** Noise grows with the largest contribution one person may make, and nearly all person-weeks hold one or two rows (72% single-row on the Amazon logs), so a cap of ten adds noise for everybody in order to leave the few heavy people unclipped. With sensitivity 1 instead of √10 the noise standard deviation is 3.2 times smaller (σ = 1.08 instead of 3.42 at ε = 4); a single-row person is not changed by unit length at all. On the confirmation windows at 1,000 people and ε = 4 the selector's list loses 0.082 instead of 0.273 (registered; doc 16's 0.308 was measured on 17 logs of other windows). At 300 and 100 people the cuts are smaller (−41% and −21% at ε = 4; post hoc).
2. **Registered result and its size.** P1–P4 hold with large margins (the thresholds were about half of the development gains, and the observed gains are 79–109% of them: +0.191 against +0.213 in development for P1, +0.185 against +0.169 for P2, +0.223 against +0.234 for P3, +0.103 against +0.130 for P4). Strong privacy still costs (ε = 2 at 1,000 people: 0.199); 100 people per week stay too few for a private weekly top 100 (price 0.274 even at ε = 8, list quality 0.245 at ε = 4 against 0.629 without privacy; post hoc).
3. **Much of the gain is tight bounding, not unit length (post hoc shares).** A hard cap of one row reaches 79–83% of the gain at 1,000 people, 65–77% at 300 and only 39–63% at 100 (section 4, point 2). What unit length adds over a one-row cap is small but clear in every case (P9 and P10 at 1,000 people: +0.022 to +0.043; post hoc at 300 people: +0.045 to +0.051). The reason is information: a one-row cap throws away every other row of a heavy person (noise-free loss 0.027 at 1,000 people), unit length keeps them with the weight 1/√r (noise-free loss 0.008). The share is a mix of a noise effect and a bias effect and depends on the order of comparison. The registered decision name says "cuts the price relative to the 10-row cap" and does not claim that unit length is special.
4. **Looser bounds look better where people have many rows and the noise is already small (description, 3 Goodreads logs at 1,000 people).** C = 2 gave a price 0.007 below C = 1 at ε = 4 and 0.010 below at ε = 8, and C = 2 was slightly better than the 10-row cap; at 300 people the 5 non-Amazon logs favour C = 1 over C = 2 at ε = 4 (+0.026 on average, positive in all 5) and not clearly at ε = 8 (+0.010, interval −0.004 to +0.025). So the best bound seems to depend on the data and the noise: C = 1 is a good default in this study, not the optimum for logs where people have many rows (untested beyond these few logs; how to choose C privately is open).
5. **Without noise the bound is almost free for unit length.** 0.008 at 1,000 people (0.0056 on the 12 Amazon logs, 0.018 on the 3 Goodreads logs, post hoc), against 0.027 for the one-row cap; P8 is met on the mean over all 15 logs, not on the Goodreads logs alone (0.018 is above the 0.015 threshold).
6. **A tight bound shortens the best memory at 1,000 people (post hoc).** Doc 16 found that noise makes the best memory longer (896 days at ε = 4 and 1,000 people). With unit-length reports the median best memory at 1,000 people is 112 days at ε = 4 and 56 days at ε = 8 (the same as without privacy at ε = 8, not at ε = 4): less noise, less need to average over many rounds. At 300 people and ε = 4 it is still 896 days with either bound, so the shortening needs enough people. The medians lie on a doubling grid and carry no interval.
7. **What this does to doc 16.** Its verdicts S9 and S11 stand: they were registered for the 10-row cap. The practical conclusion it drew ("no tested size made weekly private lists cheap") needs the qualifier "under the 10-row cap". Under unit-length reports at ε = 4 per round, 1,000 people lose 11% of the list quality (0.082 / 0.762), 300 people 37% (0.261 / 0.706) and 100 people 61% (0.384 / 0.629) (post hoc), in the central model with nominal accounting. This is not "privacy is cheap": a replace-one ε = 4 is roughly the ε = 2 column (26% at 1,000 people), the lifetime loss of a frequently drawn person is a multiple of the ε stated, and weekly lists below about 1,000 people stay far from the non-private ones.

## 5. Limits and disclosures

1. **Every price is a central-model price with nominal accounting, not a certified privacy guarantee.** A trusted server sees every person's rows; ε is per round, with no amplification by sampling, and the losses of a person who is drawn in several rounds add up; the noise sits on every item already in the catalogue (a public catalogue with known arrival dates is assumed, as in docs 12 and 16). Scaling a report to L2 norm at most C bounds the change of the summed vector by C when one person is added to or removed from a *fixed* selected set. The simulation draws a fixed number of people from the active people of each round (everyone when n or fewer are active), so adding a person also changes who else is drawn; without treating the draw as independent of the data the neighbour relation is "replace one person" and the bound is 2C, i.e. a cell labelled ε corresponds to roughly 2ε in replace-one terms (my inference from σ ∝ sensitivity/ε, not a run). The same factor applies to every sampler here, so the comparison between bounds is unaffected, but the stated ε is nominal. The number of people in a round is released without noise (it divides the counts); when n or fewer people are active it is the exact number of active people. The selector's self-score and arm choice use only the noisy counts released once per round, so they are post-processing. The sensitivity √c of the hard caps relies on one row per (person, item, round); no repeated row exists in the 27 test windows (checked), so none was removed.
2. **The bound C = 1 is a public constant**, fixed before the test and not computed from the test data. It was chosen with non-private development data from the earlier windows of the same logs (very likely the same people), which is outside the privacy accounting of the run. How to choose a bound privately when most people have many rows is open (Amin et al. 2019).
3. **Much of the gain over cap 10 comes from bounding tightly at all.** A hard cap of one row reaches 39–83% of it (section 4, point 2); unit length adds the smaller, separately tested part (P9, P10).
4. **The bias of the bound on logs with many rows per person is not tested by any hypothesis.** At 1,000 people only 3 or fewer logs are not Amazon; ml-latest (21% single-row person-weeks), ratebeer (26%) and beeradvocate (38%) are where a bound hurts a row-weighted truth most. Post-hoc splits by group are in section 4.
5. **Same 27 logs and windows as docs 14–16** (20 Amazon); the confirmation windows were used before for other questions but the DP samplers had never run on them; intervals over logs overstate independence. n = 3,000 has no confirmation cell and was not tested; rounds other than 7 days and single-use participants (the clipped sampler ignores the option) were not tested; plain sampling can draw a person many times, so the lifetime loss of frequent participants is a multiple of the ε stated (doc 16).
6. **The doc 16 prices are prices at the 10-row cap.** Doc 16 is not edited by this document except for a pointer; its verdicts S9 and S11 stand as registered.
7. **Reviews are same-provider subagents, not an outside audit.** Before the freeze: a design critic (three passes), a code reviewer and two probers on synthetic worlds; the reviewer checked the clipped sampler against a brute-force loop (540 random selections; norm never above C; adding or removing one person changes the sum by at most C), checked that the other samplers are unchanged by the patch, and that C ≤ 1 gives the same distribution as C = 1. After the run: an Opus reviewer re-implemented every hypothesis from the raw rows without importing the project code and matched every reported value (largest difference 5·10⁻⁷, the CSV rounding), the intervals, the code against the registry text, the freeze (all 18 stored sha256 values match), the noise standard deviation of the new sampler against the analytic Gaussian formula, and all 63 std-arm replications; it found that (a) a smoke cell on a test window was run before the freeze (point 11), (b) the first draft of point 8 quoted the wrong temperature limits, (c) the bootstrap intervals depend slightly on the order of the rows in `price.jsonl` (point 12), (d) the group split had to be said up front in the summary. A second Opus critic read this document against the registry and the tables and asked for the changes now in the summary, section 3 and section 5 (the replace-one factor, composition and post-processing; the scope of "mostly tight bounding"; no interval over three logs; post hoc labels; the 100-people loss). An auditor checked every number against the tables (two rounding-level or count slips fixed). The analysis was re-run and its tables are byte-identical.
8. **A 4-hour stall, then a changed governor.** The run froze from 15:32 to 19:40 because my own guard (freeze when swap exceeds 85% of its current total) was tripped by swap that other applications had filled, while more than half of the memory was free. I did not notice for hours because I was waiting on other jobs. I relaxed the guard (swap growth 6 → 12 GB, swap share 0.85 → 0.97) and later, with your consent, the fan and temperature limits: fan 1,750 → 2,400 rpm (soft) and 2,100 → 2,800 rpm (hard); die temperature 72 → 90 °C (soft) and 78 → 98 °C (hard); at most 5 workers instead of 4; one more worker allowed every 30 s instead of 120 s; cool-down wait 45 → 30 s. The governor decides only when workers run; it changes no cell value. The new values are in `configs/ssp_quiet.json`; the old ones are in git history (commit `7a24466`).
9. **One pool rebuild.** While stopping the volunteer-weights development job I terminated a process that belonged to the price run; the runner rebuilt its worker pool and re-ran the cell in flight. The one `pool rebuilt` event was kept as `price.jsonl.errors.pool-rebuilt-2026-10-16-2126` (moved aside, not deleted); the cell was re-run and passes every check. A second restart of the runner (to apply the new limits, 21:32) lost the cells in flight at that moment; they were re-run from scratch.
10. **No new material.** No download, install or execution beyond the repository's own code (doc 90).
11. **Two test cells were run before the freeze (disclosure; no hypothesis uses them).** My smoke run of the code (amazon-video-games, 100 people, window w2, 15 of the 16 samplers; it showed 0.159 for the 10-row cap and 0.378 for unit length at ε = 4) and a probe by the code reviewer on one draw of amazon-musical-instruments at 100 people. Both are at 100 people, which carries no hypothesis, and neither is in `price.jsonl`. The registry's note that the DP samplers never ran on w2 is therefore slightly too strong (the frozen file was not edited; this is the erratum), and the header of this document says "before the test cells of the final run were written".
12. **The bootstrap intervals depend slightly on the order of the rows in `price.jsonl`.** The analysis resamples logs in the order in which cells finished. With the logs sorted instead, the interval ends move by at most 0.002 (P1 upper end 0.2446 → 0.2426, P3 lower end 0.1635 → 0.1628, P4 lower end 0.0662 → 0.0658, P5 upper end 0.1131 → 0.1115); no verdict changes. The frozen analysis was not edited.
