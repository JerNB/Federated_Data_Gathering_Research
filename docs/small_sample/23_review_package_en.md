## 0. How to use this page (about 30 minutes)

1. Read section 1 (the ten claims, one line each, with their level of evidence) and pick two or three in your field.
2. In each card open the data links and do the **spot check**: one number, one file, one row (about two minutes). A spot check shows that the page quotes the table correctly; it does not re-derive the table from raw rows (section 5). `python3 scripts/ssp/check_review_numbers.py` does the same for the headline numbers, the spot checks and the table figures of every card in one go.
3. Read **Where it could be wrong** and tell us whether the weakness is bigger or smaller than we say; then the **Questions for you**.
4. Section 3 lists what failed, so you do not repeat it. Section 4 lists what we would register next and the choices still open; an outside view helps most there.

**Levels of evidence** (used in every card):

| Level | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| A | Registered before the test cells were written, tested on a window not used to develop that claim; the main registered claims were met (misses are named). |
| B | Registered before the run, but mixed, or with no separate confirmation window; misses are named. |
| C | Exploratory: a prediction was written before the run but the claim is labelled exploratory (no confirmation window), or it was found by looking. |
| D | Development: a purpose-built run or analysis on the development window or on stored cells, designed before its result was seen; nothing registered, no threshold. |
| E | A post hoc look: an exploratory analysis of stored rows of another programme, made while choosing what to do next; not designed beforehand, no development run. |

**Words used.**

- *Log*: one time-stamped public dataset (Amazon review categories, Goodreads, a beer-rating site, MovieLens and others). *Round*: a period (7, 14 or 28 days) in which n randomly drawn active people report. *Draw*: one random sample of people. *Cell*: one combination of log, people per round and round length, run with 8 draws.
- *List*: the top-100 popularity list a server publishes from a decayed sum of past rounds. *Memory*: the half-life of that decay ("2 rounds" is the short default we compare with). *Quality*: NDCG@100 of the list against the next round's full counts (1.0 is perfect).
- *Regret*: the best fixed memory of a cell, chosen with the truth, minus the rule's quality; **lower is better**. All other differences (gain, value, price comparisons) are quality differences, **higher is better**; each headline says which.
- *Self-score*: the list scored on the next round's own reporters (no ground truth). *Selector*: the rule that publishes the memory with the best recent self-score. *Table*: a rule that sets the memory from the number of people; a *same-group table* is fitted on the other logs of the same group (Amazon or other), a *pooled table* on all other logs. *Oracle*: uses information a real server would not have.
- *Windows*: w1 (development), w2 (confirmation), w3 (third), each 156 weeks per log. *Truth A* and *Truth B*: the community's full list built from the choices before and after a cut-off time T0 (the 80th percentile of choice times).
- *Central model*: a trusted server adds noise once per round. *ε* and *δ* are the usual differential-privacy numbers; *neighbouring* says which two data sets a guarantee treats as one change.
- *IDs*: registered claims keep the IDs of their registry: W (weekly), D (deep), K (confirmation), S (stress), G (guard), P (price), V (volunteer weights). A+, Ap and A− are the same-week heavy, previous-week heavy and light volunteer samplers of doc 16.

## 2. The ten cards

### RH1. The gap that matters is the community's drift, not the sample size (level C)

- **Claim.** A few hundred random people already give a list close to the community's list *at that moment*. The larger error is that the community's list moves between the time of sampling and the time it is used.
- **Why it matters.** A server should spend effort on freshness (memory, recency weights) before it asks more people.
- **Method.** 11 public datasets; random samples of N people scored (NDCG@100) against Truth A (built from the choices before T0, roughly the first 80% of a log's choices in time) and Truth B (built from the later choices); the two lists therefore span long stretches of each log's history, not single rounds. *Drift gap*: how far the two full lists are from each other. *Sampling gap*: how far a 1,000-person sample is from the full list. Also recent-window samples and recency-weighted counts. Doc 02 (X7, X10, X12); `an_explore_a.py`, `an_explore_b.py`, `an_window.py`. A descriptive follow-up in doc 21 (programme R; `an_bad_rounds.py`) uses stored cells only.
- **Result.** Against the lists themselves the drift gap is 0.658 and the sampling gap at 1,000 people is 0.003 (medians over datasets; the drift gap is larger in all 10 datasets that have a 1,000-person cell). A recent-window sample is better than a uniform one against the later list in 10 of 11 datasets (mean +0.060) and worse against the earlier list (mean −0.163). Recency-weighted counts beat plain counts in 179 of 223 cells (80%; they lose in 2), median +0.013, which closes only about 2% of the gap. Follow-up (development): at 100 and 300 people per week only 7–13% of the round-to-round variation in a sampled list's quality is draw luck (12.5% and 7.4%), and the sampled list's quality correlates 0.86 and 0.94 with the full list's, so bad rounds are shared by every draw; what causes them is not established.
- **Level and trail.** C for X7, X10 and X12: the predictions for X7 and X10 and their pass rules are in doc 01, section 7 (written 2026-10-14, before any real result was read); the `decay` estimator behind X12 was added by amendment A8, also before results were read. All three are labelled exploratory and there is no confirmation window. The fresh-seed replication in doc 02 belongs to the surprise hunt (X11), a separate scan; this page does not count it as support for these three. D for the bad-round follow-up (a purpose-built analysis of stored cells; nothing registered).
- **Data.** [`an_explore_a_x10_drift_vs_sampling.csv`](../../results/small_sample_popularity/03_tables/an_explore_a_x10_drift_vs_sampling.csv), [`an_explore_b_x7_datasets.csv`](../../results/small_sample_popularity/03_tables/an_explore_b_x7_datasets.csv), [`an_window_x12_win_share.csv`](../../results/small_sample_popularity/03_tables/an_window_x12_win_share.csv), [`badrounds_summary.csv`](../../results/small_sample_popularity/03_tables/badrounds/badrounds_summary.csv).
- **Spot check.** `an_explore_a_x10_drift_vs_sampling.csv`, the row `dataset` ml-latest, `measure` ndcg_vs_truth_lists: `drift_gap_B_minus_A` 0.65803 and `sampling_gap_N1000` 0.000909333. `badrounds_summary.csv`, the row `cadence` weekly, `n` 100: `median_within_share` 0.125, `median_rho_yf` 0.86. The sums and medians quoted above (179 of 223, 0.658, 0.003) are recomputed by the script.
- **Where it could be wrong.** 0.66 is a ceiling computed with the future answer in hand, not a measured loss (doc 03). The panel-based number is near zero partly because that metric is insensitive (0.021 against 0.0003); read the list-level number. The one-page summaries quote a dataset median (+0.037) next to a cell mean (−0.163); like with like, the cell means are +0.060 and −0.163 (doc 02) and the dataset medians are +0.037 and −0.186 (`an_explore_b_x7_datasets.csv`, `recent_window` rows). Recency weighting trades one target for the other: against the same recent window it loses in 119 of 223 cells (doc 02, X12). The bad-round numbers are descriptive: a slow trend and the list's own long memory also produce persistent quality, the correlation uses means over draws (a single server's draw correlates less; not computed), and at 30 people with months-long rounds the picture reverses (draw-to-draw share 46%, correlation 0.25).
- **Questions for you.** Is the distance between two consecutive full lists the right ceiling for "drift", or is there a better yardstick? Which temporal-evaluation baselines from the recommender literature should we compare with?
- **Next test.** A "when to ask again" rule built on a staleness measure (doc 03, next steps); RH3 is the natural alarm. Not run.

### RH2. With weekly rounds the best memory is long and shortens as people per round grow (level B)

- **Claim.** The best memory is about 32, 16, 8, 4 and 2 rounds at 30, 100, 300, 1,000 and 3,000 people per round (rule of thumb: every time n grows about 3 times, halve the memory).
- **Why it matters.** A server with weekly rounds can set its memory from its head count instead of using a short default such as "2 rounds".
- **Method.** 27 logs (20 Amazon categories, 7 others), each cut to its densest 156 weeks; rounds of 7, 14 or 28 days; a random sample of n people per round (a log is used at n if its median week has at least 3n active people: 27, 27, 24, 17, 7 logs at n = 30 … 3,000). The server publishes a top-100 list from a decayed sum of past rounds. A one-line table (n → half-life) was fitted on discovery logs only and scored on held-out logs. Doc 13; `weekly_core.py`, `run_weekly.py`, `an_weekly.py`, `an_weekly_strata.py`.
- **Result.** On held-out logs the table's regret is 0.018 against 0.118 for "2 rounds" (0.15 times as much; per n at 30 / 100 / 300 / 1,000: 0.012 / 0.018 / 0.024 / 0.019 against 0.149 / 0.171 / 0.106 / 0.047). At 100 people per week the best half-life is at least 28 days in 27 of 27 logs. Of the seven predictions frozen before the run (W1–W7) five were met; the two misses are about size, not direction (W4 +0.0042 against +0.005; W7 +0.012 against +0.02). The four written after the first results (W8–W11) were met. Also, from W11: four weekly rounds beat one 28-day round with the same number of rows by +0.022 at 100 people and +0.028 at 300.
- **Level and trail.** B. W1–W7 are in `weekly_registry.json`, committed as `7c6fab8` (2026-10-15 11:08 US Central, git author time) before any weekly run; W8–W11 were written after the first results (`weekly_registry_2.json`, `07de0a0`) and are post hoc in design.
- **Data.** [`g_memory_table.csv`](../../results/small_sample_popularity/03_tables/weekly/g_memory_table.csv), [`a_regret_by_rule.csv`](../../results/small_sample_popularity/03_tables/weekly/a_regret_by_rule.csv), [`h_strata_loo_regret.csv`](../../results/small_sample_popularity/03_tables/weekly/h_strata_loo_regret.csv), [`weekly_verdicts.json`](../../results/small_sample_popularity/03_tables/weekly/weekly_verdicts.json).
- **Spot check.** `g_memory_table.csv`, the row with `n` = 100: `table_half_life_rounds` 16, `holdout_regret_table` 0.01827, `holdout_regret_2rounds` 0.17057.
- **Where it could be wrong.** The table is Amazon-weighted. On the 7 non-Amazon logs a table fitted on the other logs is no better than "2 rounds" at 100 people (regret 0.036 against 0.030) and worse at 300 (0.047 against 0.014) and 1,000 (0.010 against 0.002); it clearly wins only at 30 people (0.014 against 0.104). On the second window the same non-Amazon pattern holds on the mean but in only 2 of 5 logs (doc 15, K8). Half-lives sit on a grid with steps of a factor 2 and the best value is at the top of the grid for many small-n logs, so the table is a lower bound there. Rounds longer than 28 days and shorter than 3 months were not tested. Drift speed and reports per person were tried as predictors in doc 14 (post hoc, leave-one-log-out): the best regression (regret 0.0127 against 0.0161 for the same-group table) mostly stands in for the domain label, its drift feature uses the full counts of the evaluation weeks, which a server does not have, and the selector (RH4) beats all of them.
- **Questions for you.** Is the effect better read as effective sample size (people times memory) or as drift speed, and how would you separate the two with these logs? Does the Amazon weighting sink the table, or only the cells with n of 100 or more outside Amazon?
- **Next test.** Separate effective sample size from drift speed with a drift measure a server can compute, and fit one table per domain. Not run.

### RH3. A server can watch its own list's quality without ground truth (level B)

- **Claim.** The score of the published list against the *next* round's own reporters tracks the list's true quality closely enough to compare settings and, averaged over draws, to flag the worst weeks after the fact.
- **Why it matters.** A server never sees the truth; this is what lets it tune itself (RH4) and, if an online version works, flag a round for review or recompute.
- **Method.** Deep programme (doc 12, D24): 245 cells over logs and people per round, Spearman correlation between the self-score and the true NDCG@100. Weekly programme (doc 13, W5 and W6): inside each of 27 logs, at 100 people and 7-day rounds with the weekly scores averaged over the 8 draws, the rank correlation over its weeks, and an alarm rule: weeks whose self-score is in the lowest 10% of the log are checked against the lowest 25% of true quality; the list uses a fixed 56-day half-life. `deep_core.py`, `weekly_core.py`, `an_weekly.py`.
- **Result.** D24 (registered threshold 0.7): rank correlation 0.95, the median of the within-group-size correlations over 4 group sizes; the pooled 0.94 over 245 cells is inflated because both scores rise with n. The self-score is about 0.41 times the true score, so it compares settings at the same n and is not an absolute number. W5 (threshold 0.5): within-log median rank correlation 0.68 (quartiles 0.56 and 0.75; 22 of 27 logs at or above 0.5; positive in all 27). W6 (threshold 40%): of 405 weeks with a lowest-10% self-score, 299 (74%) were in the lowest 25% of true quality (chance 25%).
- **Level and trail.** B. D24 is in `deep_registry.json`, drafted in `90fe827` and frozen in `f539740` (2026-10-15 01:20 US Central) before the results; W5 and W6 are in `weekly_registry.json` (`7c6fab8`, 2026-10-15 11:08). All three were met; there is no separate confirmation window and the thresholds come from the registries.
- **Data.** [`d24_spearman.csv`](../../results/small_sample_popularity/03_tables/deep/d24_spearman.csv), [`deep_verdicts.json`](../../results/small_sample_popularity/03_tables/deep/deep_verdicts.json), [`e_within_log_selfscore.csv`](../../results/small_sample_popularity/03_tables/weekly/e_within_log_selfscore.csv), [`weekly_verdicts.json`](../../results/small_sample_popularity/03_tables/weekly/weekly_verdicts.json).
- **Spot check.** `weekly_verdicts.json`, key `W6`: `alarms` 405, `hits` 299, `pooled_share` 0.7383; key `W5`: `median_rho` 0.6813. `deep_verdicts.json`, key `D24` → `numbers`: `rho_median_within_n` 0.94849, `n_values_used` 4.
- **Where it could be wrong.** W5 and W6 use means over 8 draws at 100 people and weekly rounds only, with the 56-day half-life; a real server has one draw. The 10% cut-off is the log's own decile over all its weeks, so it is set in hindsight (it includes future weeks); an online, single-draw alarm was not tested. The 10% and 25% levels were set in the registry, not tuned. The alarm is weak: a flagged week is in the worst quarter 74% of the time, not necessarily the worst decile. D24 counts cells that appear in two stages twice (219 cells after merging) and compares across logs; whether the self-score follows changes over time inside one log is what W5 tests, at one setting. In five weekly logs the scoring round itself is noisy (reliability 0.71 to 0.78, doc 21). 20 of 27 logs are Amazon.
- **Questions for you.** Is the self-score blind to a shift that moves the reporters and the truth together? Which alarm statistic would you use, how would you price false alarms, and is a single-draw online version viable?
- **Next test.** Use the alarm to decide when to ask again (doc 03 next steps; doc 04, idea 5, a 50-person probe, is only proposed and no later document reports a result for it).

### RH4. A server can pick its own memory from its own past self-scores (level A, with named misses)

- **Claim.** The server keeps a weighted average of each candidate memory's self-score (weights halve every 26 rounds) and publishes the memory with the best one. It then loses little against the best fixed memory chosen in hindsight.
- **Why it matters.** It replaces a fitted table and a domain label with a rule that needs only the server's own data.
- **Method.** The engine of RH2. Exploration on w1 (doc 14, post hoc): regret 0.0027 against 0.0161 for a same-group table, 0.0291 for a pooled table and 0.1350 for "2 rounds". Then a pre-registered confirmation on the next 156-week window w2 (doc 15; the same 27 logs; 7-day rounds are the main test). `confirm_core.py`, `run_confirm.py`, `an_confirm.py`, `explore_selfsel_weekly.py`.
- **Result.** At 7 days the selector minus the same-group table is −0.0103 (−0.0193 to −0.0039; better in 19 of 27 logs), minus the pooled table −0.0185 (−0.0283 to −0.0104; 22 of 27 logs), minus "2 rounds" −0.134. Regret above 0.01 in 2 of 90 cells (the limit was 10%). Registered misses: K11 (two short logs: 5 of 7 cells, 6 needed) and K12 (re-picking every week did not beat the memory that was best in the other window, an oracle chosen with that window's truth and mostly in the future: +0.0011). At 30 people and 28-day rounds the same-group table is better (selector +0.0081, worse in 81% of logs).
- **Level and trail.** A. `selfsel_confirm_registry.json`, committed as `8a0ef99` (2026-10-16 00:02 US Central), results first committed in `62f43bb` (02:04).
- **Data.** [`k_paired.csv`](../../results/small_sample_popularity/03_tables/confirm/k_paired.csv), [`k_summary.csv`](../../results/small_sample_popularity/03_tables/confirm/k_summary.csv), [`k_tails.csv`](../../results/small_sample_popularity/03_tables/confirm/k_tails.csv), [`k_verdicts.json`](../../results/small_sample_popularity/03_tables/confirm/k_verdicts.json), [`i_selfsel_rules.csv`](../../results/small_sample_popularity/03_tables/weekly/i_selfsel_rules.csv) (doc 14).
- **Spot check.** `k_paired.csv`, the row `round_days` 7, `scope` "w2 all", `comparison` "selector minus same": `mean_diff` −0.010311, `ci_lo` −0.019297, `ci_hi` −0.003865.
- **Where it could be wrong.** The second window is an adjacent 156-week stretch of the same 27 logs: a hold-out in time, not in population. The comparators are tables fitted leave-one-log-out, and the edge shrank from −0.0141 on the design window to −0.0103 (doc 15). 20 of the 27 logs are Amazon categories from one platform, and intervals over logs treat logs as independent, which overstates it. There are no volunteers, privacy noise or dropout in this test (they are in docs 16 and 18). The doc 14 replay found that on Amazon logs the selector is worse than "2 rounds" by more than 0.01 in 37% of cells overall and in 63% of the cells with 30 rounds per log; it needs a long history (a choice frozen after 8, 12, 20 or 40 weeks costs 0.0582, 0.0406, 0.0260 or 0.0110).
- **Questions for you.** The self-score uses the next round's reporters, who may also feed the list: can selecting on it feed back on itself? Is regret against the hindsight-best fixed memory the right yardstick, or should adaptive baselines be the comparison (a fixed-share update gave no gain, doc 17, G7)? Are leave-one-log-out tables a demanding enough comparator? What size of NDCG@100 difference is practically meaningful?
- **Next test.** RH5 and the stress tests of docs 16 and 18 are the follow-ups already run.

### RH5. Seeding the selector with the same log's self-scores from its other windows lowers its regret at 30 people (level A, for stability across eras)

- **Claim.** At 30 people per round, and with monthly rounds, the plain selector has too little data. A head start (the mean self-score each memory earned in the same log's other 156-week windows, which lie mostly *later* in time than the test window, counted as 38 pseudo-rounds that fade like normal weights) lowers its regret there, and on average does not hurt it from 100 people up. This is a statement about stability across eras, not a forecast, and the registered gate for the word "deployable" stayed closed.
- **Method.** A third window w3 never used before: 24 logs (18 Amazon), 142 cells, 8 draws each. The plain selector against the seeded one, a frozen memory and a table with a safety margin; four regions (30 people weekly or monthly; 100 or more people weekly or monthly). Doc 17; `window3_core.py`, `guard_core.py`, `run_guard.py`, `an_guard.py`.
- **Result.** Seeded minus plain regret: −0.0074 (−0.0100 to −0.0049; better in 22 of 24 logs) at 30 people with monthly rounds, and −0.0019 (−0.0027 to −0.0011; 19 of 24) at 30 people weekly. From 100 people up it is not worse on average (weekly −0.0006, monthly −0.0007; upper bounds −0.0003 and +0.0015). Nine of eleven registered claims were met; G8 (replicating doc 15's K12) and G11 (more weight on history) were not.
- **Level and trail.** A. `selfsel_guard_registry.json`, committed as `cece971` (2026-10-16 07:04 US Central); results first committed in `4c7e723` (08:08). The in-sample development gains shrank on the fresh window (for example G1 −0.0101 → −0.0074).
- **Data.** [`g_paired.csv`](../../results/small_sample_popularity/03_tables/guard/g_paired.csv), [`g_summary.csv`](../../results/small_sample_popularity/03_tables/guard/g_summary.csv), [`g_cells.csv`](../../results/small_sample_popularity/03_tables/guard/g_cells.csv), [`g_verdicts.json`](../../results/small_sample_popularity/03_tables/guard/g_verdicts.json), [`posthoc_causal.csv`](../../results/small_sample_popularity/03_tables/guard/posthoc_causal.csv).
- **Spot check.** `g_paired.csv`, the row `comparison` "warm minus plain", `region` n30_28d: `mean_diff` −0.007372 (−0.009972 to −0.004855), 24 logs. ("Warm" is the seeded selector.)
- **Where it could be wrong.** The gain is on the 18 Amazon logs: on the 6 other logs there is no gain at 30 people, and from 100 people at monthly rounds the seeded selector is worse by 0.0053 (+0.0005 to +0.0102). For 21 of the 24 logs the history windows lie later than the test window, so this tests stability across eras, not forecasting; only 3 logs allow a causal reading, so the registered gate for the word "deployable" stayed closed.
- **Questions for you.** Is seeding anything more than shrinkage toward a typical memory? A prior built from *other* logs' self-scores at the same size does nearly as well at 30 people (−0.0017 weekly and −0.0060 monthly, against −0.0019 and −0.0074). What would a clean causal test need besides new logs?
- **Next test.** A causal test on logs whose history windows come earlier than the test window; it needs new logs (a download; the decision is open, section 4).

### RH6. A small random audit repairs heavy-volunteer bias (under an oracle rule); light-volunteer bias is not shown to be repairable (level B)

- **Claim.** If volunteers fill the sample, the list is biased. A server that also asks a small random audit (max(10, n/2) people: 50 at 100 people) can score weight-and-memory choices on the audit, where the weight is a power of each person's activity. For same-week heavy volunteers (an oracle rule) this repairs the bias. For light volunteers the registered decision is "not established": they stay below random people, and the gain has an interval that includes 0.
- **Method.** Three activity-biased samplers: same-week heavy volunteers (chosen in proportion to the rows they give that week; an oracle, because a server cannot know it in advance), previous-week heavy volunteers (a rule a server could apply) and light volunteers (chosen in proportion to 1 / rows); they give 2.26, 1.16 and 0.69 times the rows per reporter of a random person. Random people are the reference, the selector is that of RH4, and the weight-and-memory choice is scored on the audit's self-score. Doc 16 (stress tests on w2: 27, 27, 21 and 15 logs at 30, 100, 300 and 1,000 people) and doc 19 (65 test cells, 24 logs); `stress_core.py`, `run_stress.py`, `an_stress.py`, `weight_core.py`, `run_vol.py`, `an_vol.py`.
- **Result.** Doc 16: all three biased samplers give a worse best fixed list than random people with the same head count (same-week heavy −0.0094, previous-week heavy −0.0037, light −0.0236). The selector keeps its regret low under both heavy rules (0.0039 and 0.0025; limit 0.006) and misses the limit under light volunteers (0.0066). Doc 19: audit-chosen weights improve same-week heavy volunteers over the uncorrected selector by +0.0124 (+0.0077 to +0.0179) and leave them within margin of random people (+0.0039, +0.0013 to +0.0065). For light volunteers the registered gain is not shown (+0.0076, −0.0009 to +0.0217) and they stay below random people (−0.0177, −0.0232 to −0.0130); the help sits on 3 of the 6 non-Amazon logs. Post hoc, at equal head count random reporters beat volunteers plus audit (100 heavy volunteers plus an audit of 50: 0.626 against 0.649 for 150 random reporters).
- **Level and trail.** B. `selfsel_stress_registry.json` (`9ab9190`, 2026-10-16 06:34; 12 of 17 entries met) and `selfsel_vol_registry.json` (`1dde943`, 2026-10-16 22:30 US Central; seven of eight positive claims met, V5 not met; of the two negative expectations V8 met and V2 not), each committed before its results (`4c7e723`, `5ffe5ae`). Two smoke cells of programme V had been run and seen before its registry was frozen (doc 19, limits).
- **Data.** [`s_verdicts.json`](../../results/small_sample_popularity/03_tables/stress/s_verdicts.json), [`s_summary.csv`](../../results/small_sample_popularity/03_tables/stress/s_summary.csv), [`v_verdicts.json`](../../results/small_sample_popularity/03_tables/volunteer/v_verdicts.json), [`v_cells.csv`](../../results/small_sample_popularity/03_tables/volunteer/v_cells.csv), [`posthoc_equal_cost.csv`](../../results/small_sample_popularity/03_tables/volunteer/posthoc_equal_cost.csv).
- **Spot check.** In `v_verdicts.json`, key `verdicts` → `V4`: `mean` 0.0124, `lo` 0.0077, `hi` 0.0179, `logs` 24. In `s_verdicts.json`, `verdicts` → `S2` → `mean_best_gain_vs_std`: `A+` −0.0094, `Ap` −0.0037, `A-` −0.0236.
- **Where it could be wrong.** A best case for the weights: volunteering was simulated as a power of a person's activity and the weight is a power of activity, so the family is close to correctly specified. The heavy-volunteer repair was measured with the same-week oracle rule only; the feasible previous-week rule was tested for the selector (doc 16) but not for the weights. The target is row-weighted popularity; under person-weighted popularity light volunteers would be unbiased (not tested). With nobody biased the audit costs −0.0056 at 30 people, beyond its margin. The audit is assumed unbiased and fully compliant, and its cost is not counted in V4–V10. Volunteers differ in activity only, never in taste. There is no multiplicity correction across the ten V claims, the S2 thresholds were written after one smoke cell, and the post hoc analyses were made after the V2 miss.
- **Questions for you.** Is activity-selected volunteering a credible stand-in for real volunteer bias, which also involves taste? Is a test-and-pool or shrinkage estimator the better design, and which published estimator should be the baseline?
- **Next test.** Realistic volunteers (taste as a dose-response) and audit size at a fixed head count: agenda items 4 and 5 of doc 21, to be developed on w1 first. RH7 comes before them.

### RH7. If the audit's people also enter the list, volunteers become nearly competitive (level D)

- **Claim.** When the 50 random audit people also feed the list (pooled), 100 heavy volunteers come within 0.0037 of 150 random reporters, and a volunteer is worth about 0.9 of a random reporter's cost (about 0.6 when the audit only scores).
- **Method.** Window w1, 27 logs; 100 heavy volunteers plus an audit of 50 against 150 random reporters; weights and memory chosen on the audit; the break-even cost ratio is the volunteer cost, relative to a random reporter, at which the two designs tie. Arms in the table: a150 = 150 random reporters, b = volunteers with the audit only scoring, c = pooled. Doc 20; `pool_core.py`, `tune_pool.py`, `check_pool.py`.
- **Result.** The shortfall against 150 random reporters falls from 0.0241 (audit only scores) to 0.0037 (pooled: −0.0037, −0.0068 to −0.0009; positive in 8 of 27 logs, within −0.005 in 18 of 27). The break-even cost ratio rises from 0.615 (0.529 to 0.692) to 0.941 (0.895 to 0.986); on a larger grid 0.89 to 0.97. Weights and memory chosen on the audit add +0.0077 (+0.0039 to +0.0118; 22 of 27 logs).
- **Level and trail.** D. Development on w1; nothing registered. Doc 20 proposes to register it first and on its own, with thresholds near half the development differences.
- **Data.** [`pilot_pool_summary.csv`](../../results/small_sample_popularity/03_tables/volunteer/pilot_pool_summary.csv), [`pilot_pool_per_log.csv`](../../results/small_sample_popularity/03_tables/volunteer/pilot_pool_per_log.csv), [`pilot_pool_grid.csv`](../../results/small_sample_popularity/03_tables/volunteer/pilot_pool_grid.csv), [`pilot_pool_overlap.csv`](../../results/small_sample_popularity/03_tables/volunteer/pilot_pool_overlap.csv).
- **Spot check.** `pilot_pool_summary.csv`, the row `block` "all 27 logs", `contrast` "c_aud - a150_sel": `mean` −0.0037, `ci_lo` −0.0068, `ci_hi` −0.0009, `logs_positive` 8.
- **Where it could be wrong.** No threshold was registered. The heavy-volunteer rule is an oracle (a server cannot know who is heavy). Every audit person answers (no non-response). The interpolation overstates the break-even in favour of volunteers, and intervals treat logs as independent.
- **Questions for you.** Is pooling an audit with a volunteer sample a known estimator whose variance can be computed instead of simulated? What rule could a server use to find heavy volunteers without an oracle?
- **Next test.** The pooled-list test, registered first and on its own, with cost ratios 1, 2/3, 1/2 and 1/3. The window (w2 or w3) is not chosen.

### RH8. Bounding each person's contribution cuts the price of privacy (level A)

- **Claim.** If every person's report is scaled to unit length (sensitivity 1) instead of capped at 10 rows (sensitivity √10), a server that adds Gaussian noise loses far less list quality; the gain is largest with many people.
- **Method.** Central model: a trusted server adds Gaussian noise once per round (ε per round, δ = 10⁻⁵, add/remove neighbours, nominal accounting, noise only on items already in the catalogue). Unit length against a 10-row cap; also a clip at C = 2 and a one-row cap; 15, 21 and 27 logs at 1,000, 300 and 100 people; window w2. *Price* is the selector value lost against plain sampling. Doc 18; `price_core.py`, `run_price.py`, `an_price.py`.
- **Result.** All ten registered claims were met. P1 (1,000 people, ε = 4): unit length beats the 10-row cap by +0.1910 (+0.1334 to +0.2446; 15 logs; the threshold was 0.10). Post hoc sizes at ε = 4: the price falls from 0.273 to 0.082 at 1,000 people, from 0.446 to 0.261 at 300 and from 0.484 to 0.384 at 100. Most of this is bounding tightly: a one-row cap alone reaches 79–83% of the gain at 1,000 people, 65–77% at 300 and 39–63% at 100. What unit length adds over a one-row cap at equal sensitivity is smaller but registered: +0.0319 (+0.0249 to +0.0398; P9) at 1,000 people and ε = 4, and +0.0425 and +0.0218 at ε = 2 and 8 (P10). Strong privacy still costs: ε = 2 at 1,000 people costs 0.199, and 100 people per week are too few for a private weekly top 100.
- **Level and trail.** A. `selfsel_price_registry.json`, committed as `d811aae` (2026-10-16 13:48 US Central); results first committed in `5a03975` (22:30). Thresholds were set near half the development gains.
- **Data.** [`p_verdicts.json`](../../results/small_sample_popularity/03_tables/price/p_verdicts.json), [`p_cells.csv`](../../results/small_sample_popularity/03_tables/price/p_cells.csv), [`p_summary.csv`](../../results/small_sample_popularity/03_tables/price/p_summary.csv), [`posthoc_price_list.csv`](../../results/small_sample_popularity/03_tables/price/posthoc_price_list.csv), [`posthoc_share_of_gain.csv`](../../results/small_sample_popularity/03_tables/price/posthoc_share_of_gain.csv).
- **Spot check.** `p_verdicts.json`, key `verdicts` → `P1`: `mean` 0.191, `lo` 0.1334, `hi` 0.2446, `logs` 15. `posthoc_price_list.csv`, the rows `n` 1000, `eps` 4: `price_mean` 0.2734 for `cap10` and 0.0824 for `clip1` (unit length).
- **Where it could be wrong.** The headline compares with a 10-row cap, a weak baseline; the one-row comparison is the fair one. These are not certified guarantees: the model is central, ε is per round and adds up for a person drawn in several rounds, and a replace-one reading of "neighbouring" doubles the sensitivity. The bound was chosen with non-private development data from earlier windows of the same logs. At 1,000 people the gain comes from the Amazon logs (Goodreads: +0.018 at ε = 2, −0.003 at ε = 4, −0.009 at ε = 8). n = 3,000 has no confirmation cell; intervals over logs overstate independence.
- **Questions for you.** Is the sensitivity bookkeeping right (unit-length reports, analytic Gaussian noise at δ = 10⁻⁵, no amplification by sampling)? Is a 10-row cap a fair baseline? Is "noise grows with the largest contribution" the whole story of the gain?
- **Next test.** Choosing the bound privately and L1 against L2 normalisation (doc 18, next steps); RH9 develops part of it.

### RH9. Under a lifetime privacy count, one draw per person at the full budget beats several draws at a fraction, and Laplace noise beats Gaussian most when each person is drawn once (level D)

- **Claim.** Count privacy over a person's lifetime (the 156 weeks of a cell). How often a person is drawn then matters more than which noise is used: where the pool does not run out (the cap binds in ratebeer, beeradvocate and ml-latest), one draw at the full budget loses far less list quality than five draws at a fraction of it, with either noise, even though the single draw has the smaller lifetime ε (8 against 10–10.4). And pure-DP Laplace noise (δ = 0) beats Gaussian (δ = 10⁻⁵) by the most when each person is drawn once (about 3 times the people at ε = 4), by about 1.2–1.5 times at five draws and 1.0–1.25 times at ten.
- **Method.** Window w1; 100, 300 and 1,000 people (27, 24 and 17 logs; 68 cells, 8 draws each, 136 with the single-use set). Laplace reports are scaled to L1 norm 1 and Gaussian reports to L2 norm 1 (RH8). A cap on how often one person can be drawn (1, 5 or 10 draws), with Laplace ε set so the lifetime guarantee equals the Gaussian's. Docs 21 and 22; `lap_core.py`, `tune_lap.py`, `check_lap.py`, `cap_core.py`, `tune_cap.py`, `check_cap.py`, `an_exposure.py`.
- **Result.** At 100 people, one draw at ε = 8 loses 17% (Laplace) or 49% (Gaussian) of the plain selector's value; five draws at about the same lifetime privacy (Laplace ε = 2, Gaussian ε = 4) lose 63% and 65%; the pattern holds at 300 and 1,000 people. Per round at ε = 4 the share lost falls from 66% to 41% (100 people), 42% to 15% (300) and 13% to 4% (1,000). One draw per person at the same ε: Laplace minus Gaussian +0.156 (+0.124 to +0.187), +0.187 (+0.142 to +0.229), +0.064 (+0.038 to +0.092), about 3.1 and 3.0 times the people at 100 and 300. Five draws at equal lifetime privacy: +0.007 to +0.058 over six contrasts (four of six intervals above 0), about 1.2–1.5 times; ten draws: −0.019 to +0.030, about 1.0–1.25 times. With the same one-row signal in both noises the Laplace gain at ε = 4 is +0.190, +0.228 and +0.091, so the gain is the noise and not the L1 scaling. The cap rarely binds: the median number of people per round is unchanged, and it binds in three logs (at one draw and 300 people ratebeer keeps only 28% of its people); the 36 noisy cap-cost contrasts lie between −0.016 and +0.011. The L1 bound C = 1 beats C = 2 and C = 4.
- **Level and trail.** D. Development on w1; nothing registered. The single-use contrast was chosen after a partial look at 33 of 68 cells (disclosed in doc 22, section 1). Doc 22 proposes a privacy family on w3: five directional contrasts with thresholds near half the development gains and 99% bootstrap intervals (Bonferroni over five).
- **Data.** [`cap_dev_grid.csv`](../../results/small_sample_popularity/03_tables/price/cap_dev_grid.csv), [`cap_dev_paired.csv`](../../results/small_sample_popularity/03_tables/price/cap_dev_paired.csv), [`cap_dev_equivalents.csv`](../../results/small_sample_popularity/03_tables/price/cap_dev_equivalents.csv), [`laplace_dev_paired.csv`](../../results/small_sample_popularity/03_tables/price/laplace_dev_paired.csv), [`laplace_dev_lifetime.csv`](../../results/small_sample_popularity/03_tables/price/laplace_dev_lifetime.csv).
- **Spot check.** `cap_dev_paired.csv`, the row `kind` equal_lifetime_capped, contrast "k=1: Laplace eps 4 minus Gaussian eps 4", `n` 100: `mean` 0.15635, `ci_lo` 0.1244, `ci_hi` 0.18739, `logs_positive` 26 (of 27 logs). `cap_dev_grid.csv`, the rows `n` 100: `lost_share_of_std_selector` 0.1726 for `lapn:eps=8,C=1,kmax=1` and 0.6548 for `gaussn:eps=4,C=1,kmax=5`.
- **Where it could be wrong.** The lifetime numbers depend on the neighbouring relation (add or remove a person, with who is active and who is drawn public, a public catalogue, a trusted server). Under a replace-one relation the single-use Laplace gain at 100 people lies between +0.025 and +0.098 instead of +0.156 (inferred from existing arms, not simulated). Per round at ε = 4 the Laplace gain is smaller outside Amazon: +0.102, +0.038 and −0.004 at 100, 300 and 1,000 people (Amazon logs +0.167, +0.244, +0.089; doc 21). "Lifetime" is the 156 weeks of a cell; a system that runs longer exhausts any fixed cap. The people-equivalents (about 3 times and so on) are interpolations over the 17 logs present at every size, which exclude the three logs where the cap binds. At equal ε per round δ = 0 is compared with δ = 10⁻⁵, which is conservative for Laplace per round and favours the Gaussian over many releases. The catalogue is data-dependent and noise sits on fewer items than a truly public catalogue would have, so absolute prices are optimistic. The samplers run in floating point, and textbook floating-point Laplace noise is not exactly pure DP. One window, one seed, 8 draws per cell; 20 of 27 logs are Amazon.
- **Questions for you.** Which neighbouring relation would you use for a popularity list, and what is the Laplace advantage under it? Is a floating-point-safe Laplace implementation needed for any claim here? Is "one draw at the full budget" a realistic participation rule for a deployed system?
- **Next test.** The w3 privacy family above (decisions open, section 4), after a replace-one column is built. Its five contrasts compare Laplace with Gaussian; the one-draw against five-draw comparison is not among them.

### RH10. A private top 10 needs far fewer people than a private top 100 (level E)

- **Claim.** With noise added to every item, a short list keeps much more of its noise-free quality than a long one: at ε = 4 a weekly top 10 keeps about 80% from 300 people, while a top 100 needs about 1,000.
- **Method.** A first look at the stored rows of RH8 (doc 18, section 4, point 6), cutting the published list at 10, 30, 100 and 300 items with the best fixed memory chosen in hindsight at each cut; unit-length reports; window w2. It was a look made while choosing the next programme, not a test. `explore_price_k.py`.
- **Result.** At ε = 4 a top 10 keeps 0.49, 0.79 and 0.96 of its noise-free quality at 100, 300 and 1,000 people, a top 100 keeps 0.42, 0.64 and 0.89. Cutting to the top 10 helps most at 300 people (kept at top 10 minus kept at top 100: +0.158, +0.122 to +0.193, positive in all 21 logs).
- **Level and trail.** E. Post hoc; no registry; no development run. A fresh window is the first requirement.
- **Data.** [`posthoc_list_length.csv`](../../results/small_sample_popularity/03_tables/price/posthoc_list_length.csv), [`posthoc_list_length_head_vs_tail.csv`](../../results/small_sample_popularity/03_tables/price/posthoc_list_length_head_vs_tail.csv), [`posthoc_price_list.csv`](../../results/small_sample_popularity/03_tables/price/posthoc_price_list.csv).
- **Spot check.** `posthoc_list_length.csv`, the rows `n` 300, `eps` 4, `sampler` clip1: `retained` 0.794 for `k` 10 and 0.642 for `k` 100.
- **Where it could be wrong.** The 80% rule of thumb is read off a table, not registered. The same limits as RH8 apply (central model, nominal accounting, Amazon-heavy logs, intervals over logs). The best memory is chosen in hindsight at each cut. A literature agent's reading (not verified by us) is that no published mechanism beats noise on every item for a public catalogue and 100–1,000 users, and that no published number says how many users a private top 10 needs.
- **Questions for you.** Is there a published private top-k mechanism for a public catalogue and at most 1,000 users that beats per-item noise? Is "kept quality at the cut" the right measure for a top 10?
- **Next test.** A development run on w1 for the top 10, then a registered test on a fresh window (doc 21, agenda item 6).

## 3. What did not survive (so you do not repeat it)

- Main study (doc 02): breadth beats depth, inconclusive (61% of 46 pairs, +0.0005 NDCG@100); a hybrid chosen inside the sample, falsified (within 0.002 of the better pure list in 70% of 132 cells, worse by 0.0018 on average); the crossover size claim, mixed.
- A subgroup that differs more from the global list crosses over at a smaller sample, not a larger one: our registered direction was backwards (Spearman −0.44; −0.20 after removing the series that never cross).
- Two-round asking and last-year's season list gave no useful gain (doc 05).
- The tournament (doc 09): nine of the first twelve ideas were killed at the screening stage (H1–H6 and H10–H12).
- Doc 12: none of eight alternatives to the half-life-2 decayed sum gained; a rotating panel does nothing; asking heavy users more often is an oracle and not usable; a minimum-count threshold on published items destroys a 100-person server.
- The statistically unbiased weighted count never beat the plain count, or beat it only at samples of 10% of the group or more, in 47 of 50 dataset-and-k series (X15, doc 02).
- Registered misses inside the programmes above: S2, S3c, S4c, S9 and S11 (doc 16); K11 and K12 (doc 15); G8 and G11 (doc 17); V2 and V5 (doc 19); W4 and W7 (doc 13).
