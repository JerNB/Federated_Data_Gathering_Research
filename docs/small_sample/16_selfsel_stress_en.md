# 16. Does the self-score selector survive volunteers, privacy noise and churn? (pre-registered stress tests)

*Pre-registered: the plan `configs/selfsel_stress_registry.json` (17 hypotheses S1–S13, thresholds, family rules) was committed in `9ab9190` before any cell was run. Verdicts are computed by `scripts/ssp/an_stress.py`. Anything marked "post hoc" was not in the plan. Tables: `results/small_sample_popularity/03_tables/stress/`.*

## 0. Summary

1. **12 of the 17 registered claims were met and 5 were not.** The four family flags: *robust to volunteer bias* — **no** (S3c); *an audit sample is useless* — **no**, not shown (S4c); *choosing the memory cuts the price of privacy* — **no**: the price fell by 30%, not by half (S9); *switching memory is cheap* — **yes** (S12, S13).
2. **Volunteers (family B).** When heavy users volunteer, the selector keeps beating the same-group table (same-week heavy users −0.0067, previous-week heavy users −0.0130 NDCG regret; both intervals below 0). When light users volunteer, the edge is −0.0064 with an interval from −0.0159 to +0.0021, so it is not established, and the mean regret (0.0066) is just above the registered limit of 0.006 (S3c not met). By group size (post hoc): no edge at 30 people (+0.0006); from 100 people the point estimates are −0.0078, −0.0137 and −0.0156 at 100, 300 and 1,000 people (the intervals end at −0.0002, −0.0029 and +0.0001). This is the same picture as doc 15: the selector is a tool from about 100 people per round.
3. **One registered expectation was not met (S2).** I expected heavy-user volunteers to help. They send 2.3 times more rows per reporter, yet the best fixed list is *worse* than with random people by 0.0094 per cell (previous-week heavy users −0.0037; light users −0.0236). Per log the same-week heavy effect is −0.0075 with an interval from −0.0164 to +0.0005, so on its own it is not clearly below 0. Post hoc: they help at 30 people (+0.0076, interval includes 0) and hurt from 100 people (−0.0125 at 100, −0.0201 at 300, −0.0195 at 1,000), and much more on the 7 non-Amazon logs (−0.0214) than on the 20 Amazon logs (−0.0027).
4. **A reading that fits (post hoc check, section 4.3).** Picking people in proportion to their rows, and taking all their rows, over-weights heavy people relative to the truth, which counts each row once; that matters only because heavy and light people like different items. The expected counts of such samples, computed without any sampling noise, reproduce the loss for light volunteers (−0.022 and −0.020 expected against −0.018 and −0.018 observed at 300 and 1,000 people; rank correlation across logs +0.74 and +0.91). For same-week heavy volunteers the expected bias is also negative and ranks the logs in the right order (+0.61 and +0.70) but is 1.8 to 2.8 times larger than what was observed; the extra rows that heavy reporters bring cut the sampling noise, which is the likely offset (not tested separately).
5. **An audit sample (n/2 random people) cannot be called useless, nor useful.** It does not help when heavy users volunteer (+0.0005 and −0.0010 per cell). When light users volunteer it gains +0.0020 per cell (+0.0032 per log, interval −0.0003 to +0.0096; post hoc: mostly at 30 and 100 people). It costs +0.0015 when nobody is biased (S5, met).
6. **Privacy (family D; central model with a trusted server, ε per round, a public catalogue; section 5).** Noise lengthens the best memory: at ε = 4 it was at least twice the noise-free memory in 23 of 24 cells (95.8%; registered), and the median factor is 8 (post hoc: 16 at ε = 2, 4 at ε = 8). The "2 rounds" default loses 0.183 of regret at ε = 4 and 1,000 people. The selector finds the memory under noise (regret 0.0084; −0.0158 against a pooled table, interval −0.0306 to −0.0016), although at ε = 2 and 1,000 people tables beat it (post hoc, section 3.6).
7. **The price of privacy stays large.** At ε = 4 and 1,000 people the selector's list loses 0.308 NDCG against the same selector without noise (0.752 → 0.444); the "2 rounds" default loses 0.438. The selector's price is 30% below the default's, not half (S9 not met); only 29% of the 17 logs got it to half. Weak privacy is not free either: ε = 8 with 3,000 people still costs 0.051 (registered: at most 0.03; S11 not met). These are prices *at the registered 10-row cap*, not the lowest price (section 5, point 3).
8. **Single-use participants cost nothing measurable at 1,000 people (S10 met).** If nobody is drawn twice, ε is the person's whole loss rather than a per-round loss; the selector's list changed by +0.0013 NDCG on average (about ±0.01 per cell). At 3,000 people the pool runs short in some windows (post hoc: about 0.005 worse).
9. **Churn.** The selector's published top 100 changes by 12% from one week to the next, about the same as the best fixed memory (+0.0055; limit 0.03) and far less than the "2 rounds" default (57%); it is steadier than the default in 98% of cells.
10. **Reading for practice (post hoc, not registered).** (a) Volunteer bias can cost more than the selector can lose: with same-week heavy or light volunteers the best list is 0.0125–0.0250 NDCG worse at 100+ people, against a selector regret below 0.006 (previous-week heavy volunteers, the only rule a server could apply without an oracle, cost only 0.0015–0.0035). Fix who is asked, not the selector. **Doc 19 (programme V, added later) adds a partial repair: weights chosen on a small random audit sample gain +0.0124 NDCG over the uncorrected selector under same-week heavy volunteers (100–300 people) and do not repair light volunteers (pre-registered; simulated volunteers).** (b) Under the 10-row cap, no tested size made weekly private lists cheap: 1,000 people at ε = 4 lose 41% of the list quality, and 3,000 people at ε = 8 still lose 7%. **Doc 18 (programme P, added later) shows that this was largely the price of the cap: with every person's report scaled to unit length the selector's price at 1,000 people and ε = 4 falls from 0.273 to 0.082 on the confirmation windows (pre-registered; central model, nominal accounting).** (c) If privacy is required, single-use participants make the stated ε true for each person, while the pool lasts.

## 1. What was done

1. **Same engine, new conditions.** Every stress cell is a stored cell of an earlier run (same log, window, number of people, seeds), with extra arms added. The plain-sampling arms of every stress cell reproduce the stored cell exactly; the analysis stops if they do not (checked: 162 of 162).
2. **Family B, volunteers and an audit sample** (window w2 of docs 14–15, 7-day rounds, 30/100/300/1,000 people, 90 cells; 27, 27, 21 and 15 logs). Three ways of choosing who reports, always `n` people per round, who send all their rows:
   - **A+ (same-week heavy volunteers).** The chance of being picked is proportional to the person's rows in that week (an oracle: the server would have to know it in advance).
   - **Ap (previous-week heavy volunteers).** The chance is proportional to the person's rows in the week before (feasible; 1 for new people).
   - **A− (light volunteers).** The chance is proportional to 1 / rows.
   Every cell also draws, independently, an **audit sample** of max(10, n/2) random active people per round; the audit self-score is the list's score against the audit sample instead of the reporters.
3. **Family D, privacy noise** (window w1 of doc 13, 7-day rounds, 1,000 people in 17 logs and 3,000 people in 7 logs, 24 cells). Central Gaussian noise (analytic Gaussian, δ = 10⁻⁵, at most 10 random rows per person, so L2 sensitivity √10) added to the count of every item already in the catalogue, ε = 2, 4, 8 per round; **single-use participants** (nobody drawn twice) at ε = 4; and a **capped noise-free reference** (ε = 10⁶, same 10-row cap) so that the loss from the cap and the loss from the noise can be told apart. The **price** of a rule is its NDCG without noise minus its NDCG with noise.
4. **Family C, churn** (window w2, 100 and 300 people, 48 cells): the overlap of each published top 100 with the previous one was stored. Churn = 1 − the share of the top 100 kept.
5. **Pre-registration.** 17 hypotheses with thresholds taken from docs 12–15 and from quantities computed on the plain-sampling data (rows per reporter; no duplicate (person, item, week) rows in the 17 privacy windows; the fill of the single-use pool). Three design critics and three code reviewers (same provider) shaped the plan before the freeze; their changes: a per-sampler manipulation check, the log (not the cell) as the unit for S3 and S8, a confidence-interval condition in S4, the capped noise-free reference for the price of privacy, a fill guard for S10, a not-evaluable rule for S6.
6. **Run.** 162 cells (48 churn, 90 volunteers, 24 privacy) under the quiet governor on 2026-10-16 (06:34–07:11), no failed cell, no cell dropped. The registered files are unchanged since the freeze commit.

## 3. Interpretation

1. **S3c not met; otherwise the selector keeps finding the right memory when people volunteer.** Regret is measured against the best fixed memory *of the same sampler*, so S3 says "it still picks well", not "the lists are as good". Under heavy volunteers its regret is 0.0039 (A+) and 0.0025 (Ap); on the same cells that is 0.0017 and 0.0001 above its regret under random people (the information item of S3), and its edge over the table is −0.0067 and −0.0130. Under light volunteers the regret is 0.0066 (0.0031 above random people) and the edge has an interval that crosses 0; at 30 people the regret is 0.0100 with no edge (+0.0006), and from 100 people the point estimates are −0.0078 and better (the interval at 100 people ends at −0.0002). Ap is almost the same sampler as random people on the 20 Amazon logs (1.02 times the rows per reporter), so S3b and S4b are in practice a test on the 7 other logs and on small groups.
2. **Who volunteers matters more than which memory is chosen.** Light volunteers cost 0.0236 of list quality and same-week heavy volunteers 0.0094 (S2) before the selector does anything; the selector's own regret averages 0.0066 or less under each volunteer rule (0.0100 at 30 people with light volunteers). Repairing the selector is the wrong lever; repairing the sample is the right one.
3. **The registered heavy-volunteer expectation was not met, and the data fit a bias-versus-noise trade.** I had reasoned (Hansen–Hurwitz style, derived and not sourced) that picking people by their rows makes an uncorrected count unbiased for the row-weighted truth. A Hansen–Hurwitz or Horvitz–Thompson estimator weights each drawn unit by 1 / (its probability); an uncorrected count of all rows of the drawn people is not that estimator (doc 90, section 6, item 5). In the small-sample limit a heavy person is counted in proportion to rows²; at 300–1,000 people the heavy users are drawn almost for sure, so the effective weighting is weaker (section 4.3). The bias exists only because heavy and light people like different items: if everyone had the same item distribution, over-weighting heavy people would change nothing. That is the second ingredient, and it is a property of the data, not of the sampler. It is stronger on the 7 non-Amazon logs, where a reporter has 5.9 rows per week on average against 1.6 on the Amazon logs (−0.0214 against −0.0027). More rows per reporter help when the sample is tiny (30 people: +0.0076, interval includes 0) and the bias wins once noise is small (100+ people: −0.0125 to −0.0201). The deterministic check in section 4.3 reproduces the loss from both ingredients together; it cannot separate them, and the offset from the extra rows was not tested. A stricter test would give heavy and light people the same item distribution (for example by shuffling the items among rows within a round) and show that the expected loss vanishes; that is true by construction, so it was not run. The smoke cell that preceded the registry showed +0.034 for A+ (averaged over all arms of one cell, n = 100); it was a single cell and misleading.
4. **An audit is a hedge against light volunteers, not a cure.** The 95% interval of S4c reaches 0, so "helps only when light users volunteer" is not a registered finding; what holds is "an audit was not shown to be useless under light volunteers" (mean gain 0.0020 per cell, at most about 0.01). Under the two heavy-volunteer rules the reporters outnumber the audit and are no worse than the audit for choosing the memory (S4a, S4b).
5. **Noise makes memory longer; the selector finds it.** Without noise, the median best memory is 56 days at 1,000 people and 14 days at 3,000. With noise it is 224 days (ε = 8), 896 days (ε = 4, 2) at 1,000 people and 56, 112, 224 days at 3,000 (post hoc). At 1,000 people the best memory is the longest one on the grid in 59% (ε = 2) and 53% (ε = 4) of logs, so the real optimum is at least that long and the ratios above are lower bounds; a 896-day half-life is already nearly an average over the whole 156-week window.
6. **Privacy is expensive here, and tables compete with the selector at 1,000 people.** Price list for the selector (list quality lost against the same selector without noise). Registered: the ε = 4 / 1,000-people entries for the selector and the default (S9), and the ε = 2 / 1,000-people and ε = 8 / 3,000-people entries for the selector (S11); the other entries are post hoc.

   | | ε = 2 | ε = 4 | ε = 8 |
   |---|---:|---:|---:|
   | selector, 1,000 people (17 logs) | 0.456 | 0.308 | 0.163 |
   | selector, 3,000 people (7 logs) | 0.262 | 0.128 | 0.051 |
   | "2 rounds" default, 1,000 people | 0.523 | 0.438 | 0.329 |
   | "2 rounds" default, 3,000 people | 0.411 | 0.282 | 0.140 |

   At ε = 4 and 1,000 people, the noise-free best list scores 0.752, the "2 rounds" default 0.270 with noise, the selector 0.444 and the best fixed memory (chosen with the truth) 0.452. So the selector collects 0.174 of the 0.182 that a better memory can win over the default (95%). The other 0.30 (0.752 → 0.452) is not removed by any memory on the tested grid; since the best memory is at the top of the grid in 53% of logs, a longer memory could recover a little of it. The loss caused by the 10-row cap alone is 0.0006. The 30% (S9) is measured against the "2 rounds" default; a fixed memory from a table recovers much of the same gain. Regret of each rule at 1,000 people (post hoc):

   | | "2 rounds" | selector | pooled table | same-group table |
   |---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
   | ε = 2 | 0.1297 | 0.0185 | 0.0099 | 0.0059 |
   | ε = 4 | 0.1827 | 0.0084 | 0.0242 | 0.0107 |
   | ε = 8 | 0.2131 | 0.0031 | 0.0346 | 0.0197 |

   At ε = 2 the tables beat the selector (the selector minus the pooled table is +0.0086, interval −0.0040 to +0.0215); at ε = 4 and 8 the selector is better than the pooled table (−0.0158 and −0.0315) and than the same-group table. At 3,000 people (7 logs) the selector's regret is 0.0041, 0.0012 and −0.0003 against 0.042–0.070 for the two tables.
7. **Single-use participants are free at 1,000 people, while the pool lasts.** The registered privacy loss ε is per round. Under plain sampling a person can be drawn again: the expected number of draws is 0.07–0.70 on average per window but 5.9–34.5 for the most active people (maximum over the 17 windows; post hoc), so the lifetime loss of frequent participants is a multiple of the ε stated. With single-use participants the stated ε is the whole loss, and the list quality did not change (+0.0013). At 3,000 people the pool of unused people runs short in some windows (post hoc: the single-use selector was about 0.005 worse at ε = 4), and over a long enough run any pool is used up.
8. **Switching memory does not make the list jumpy.** The 57% weekly churn of the "2 rounds" default likely comes from noise in small samples rather than from adaptivity (no comparison isolates the cause): the selector's lists move by 12%, as much as the best fixed memory's (11.6%). S13 alone would be a weak test (the default is very jumpy); S12 carries the claim.

## 5. Limits and disclosures

1. **Not an independent sample of the world.** The same 27 logs (20 Amazon) and the windows of docs 13–15; the intervals over logs treat logs as independent, which overstates independence. Family D at 3,000 people rests on 7 logs.
2. **Simulated reporters and stylised volunteers.** The three volunteer rules use activity only, never taste, and A+ and A− are extremes; real volunteers differ in taste as well as activity. The truth is row-weighted popularity; person-weighted popularity (every person counts once) was not tested, and under it A− would be unbiased.
3. **The privacy model is a simplification, not a certified guarantee.** Central model (a trusted server sees every person's rows); neighbouring data = one person added or removed; the Gaussian noise goes on every item already in the catalogue (a public catalogue with known arrival dates is assumed, as in doc 12; a catalogue learned privately would need a thresholding step that doc 12 showed to be harsh on small servers); ε is per round with no composition and no amplification by sampling; the choice of who takes part is treated as independent of the data; at most 10 rows per person. The zero-duplicate check (no (person, item, week) pair twice) was made for the 17 windows of family D only. **The 10-row cap was inherited from doc 12 and was not tuned.** Because noise scales with the largest contribution a person may make, a cap of 10 adds noise for every person even though most persons have one or two rows. The prices in section 3 are therefore prices at that cap, not the lowest price; doc 18 tests tighter bounds and finds much lower prices.
4. **Family D runs on the window where the selector was designed** (w1); the plain-sampling side of those cells was analysed in docs 13–14.
5. **Disclosures from the registry.** Four smoke cells were run before the freeze (amazon-musical-instruments volunteers at 30 and 100 people and churn at 100 people on w2; amazon-cell-phones-and-accessories privacy at 1,000 people on w1); the S2 thresholds (±0.01) were written after seeing one smoke cell (A+ +0.034, A− −0.023 against random people, averaged over all arms of that cell); S7 and S11 were written knowing doc 12's central-DP prices with a 2-round memory (0.45, 0.39, 0.31, 0.20 for ε 1, 2, 4, 8 at 1,000 people); the engine edits were uncommitted until the freeze commit.
6. **The registry's literature note was wrong in one place.** Its point (2) said that picking people in proportion to their rows leaves a count unbiased for the row-weighted truth (Hansen–Hurwitz); that holds only if each picked person contributes one row, which is not what the simulation does (section 3, point 3). The registered S2 expectation followed from it. The file is hash-frozen and was not edited; this paragraph is the erratum.
7. **S4c's rule text would overclaim.** The registry says that if S4c alone fails, the write-up says "an audit helps only when light users volunteer". The numbers do not show that: the mean passes (+0.0020, just under 0.002) and the interval upper bound fails (+0.0096), with the interval including 0. This document uses the wording of section 3, point 4.
8. **S10 is met on the mean only.** The mean absolute per-cell difference is 0.0099, close to the ±0.01 band.
9. **Reviews are same-provider subagents, not an outside audit.** An Opus reviewer re-implemented every hypothesis from the raw rows without importing the project code and matched every reported value (to 5 decimals), the seven bootstrap intervals it recomputed, the code against the registry text (17 of 17), the freeze (`git diff` empty; all stored sha256 values match), two hand-computed cells and four std-arm replications. The analysis was re-run once more and its tables are byte-identical. The reviewer found the S2 title, the S4c rule text and the "cuts the price" wording misleading; a second Opus critic read this document against the registry and the tables and asked for the changes now in sections 0, 3 and 5 (the two ingredients of the volunteer bias, the table comparison next to S9, the privacy-model caveats, registered versus post hoc labels).
10. **No new material.** No download, install or execution beyond the repository's own code (doc 90).

## 6. What comes next

1. **Programme G** (doc 17): seeding or guarding the selector for the weak regions (30 people; monthly rounds), tested on a third window.
2. **Programme P (done, doc 18): cut the price of privacy by bounding each person's contribution** (fewer rows per person; norm clipping instead of a hard cap), motivated by Amin et al. 2019, Epasto et al. 2020 and Bagdasaryan et al. 2022 (doc 90, section 6). Tuned on window w1, pre-registered, tested on w2: all 10 hypotheses met.
3. **Programme V (done, doc 19): correct the volunteer bias** with inverse-probability-style weights (Horvitz–Thompson / Hájek) whose exponent is chosen on an audit sample. Section 4.3 gave the size of the bias to be removed. Result: 7 of the 8 positive claims met; heavy volunteers gain, light volunteers are not brought up to random people.
4. **Left open:** person-weighted popularity, volunteers who differ in taste, a recurring panel, composition when a person is drawn many times, local DP, private top-k mechanisms with an unknown catalogue.
