## 0. Summary

1. **Of the 8 registered claims other than the two negative expectations (V1, V3–V7, V9, V10; V3 is a registered null), 7 were met and V5 was not. Of the 2 registered negative expectations, V8 was met (the method fails for light volunteers) and V2 was not (weights helped light volunteers more than the null allowed).** The registry reports V2 and V8 apart from the count. Decisions by their registered rules: audit-chosen weights repair heavy volunteers — **yes** (V1, V4, V7, V10); they are safe under random sampling — **yes** (V3, V6, on the pooled 100- and 300-people mean); "light volunteers are not repaired" — **not established by its rule** (V2 was not met; V8 was met: light volunteers stay below random people).
2. **Heavy volunteers: a gain that about equals the loss where there is one.** Same-week heavy volunteers (the chance of reporting is proportional to a person's rows; an oracle rule) give the audit-weighted selector **+0.0124 (+0.0077 to +0.0179)** over the uncorrected selector (V4; the best exponent chosen with the truth would give +0.0134, V1; the audit's choice is within 0.0032 of the best arm, V9). The weights carry the gain: +0.0123 (+0.0079 to +0.0173) over the audit selector without weights (V10); the audit's choice of memory alone adds about 0 (+0.0001, −0.0006 to +0.0008; post hoc), as doc 16 found (S4). With weights, heavy volunteers are not worse than random people with the same number of reporters (V7, a non-inferiority claim; observed +0.0039, +0.0013 to +0.0065). The loss they cause is itself modest (post hoc): +0.0063 at 100 people (−0.0006 to +0.0137), +0.0118 at 300. Split by the observed sign of the loss (so indicative only): in 15 logs heavy volunteers lose (mean loss 0.0169, mean gain 0.0174); in 9 they were already ahead of random people (mean loss −0.0055) and end 0.0096 above them, which is where most of V7's +0.0039 comes from. Counting the audit's 50 people, heavy volunteers lose to 150 random reporters by 0.020 (point 6).
3. **How the audit helps (post hoc).** Under heavy volunteers it picks the exponent β = 0.5 (rows weighted by rows^(−0.5), which shrinks heavy reporters) in 64% of rounds at 100 people and 75% at 300, against 25% and 21% when the reporters are random people, and a negative exponent almost never. A wrong-direction exponent held fixed would cost 0.115 NDCG at 100 people and 0.125 at 300 (β = −1), so the choice matters.
4. **Random people: a small, real cost.** The audit-weighted selector scores −0.0015 (−0.0021 to −0.0008) against the uncorrected one when nobody is biased (V6; margin 0.005): inside the margin, with an interval below 0. Post hoc: −0.0021 at 100 people, −0.0008 at 300 and **−0.0056 (−0.0074 to −0.0038) at 30 people, beyond the margin** (the audit has 15 people there; under heavy volunteers at 30 people the gain is +0.0030, with an interval of −0.0026 to +0.0100 that includes 0).
5. **Light volunteers: not brought up to random people.** The audit-weighted selector stays 0.0177 below random people (−0.0232 to −0.0130; V8 met; doc 16's bias-only floor for light volunteers is about −0.02). Weights did help in hindsight beyond the null (V2: +0.0067, +0.0003 to +0.0170, not met) and the audit-weighted gain is +0.0076 with an interval that reaches below 0 (−0.0009 to +0.0217; V5 not met). Post hoc: the help sits on 3 of the 6 non-Amazon logs (ml-latest +0.1056, goodreads-comics +0.0289, goodreads-children +0.0190 in hindsight); the 6 non-Amazon logs together give +0.0259 in hindsight and +0.0341 audit-weighted, the 18 Amazon logs +0.0002 and −0.0012. The 24-log V2 mean passes 0.005 only because of ml-latest (without it +0.0023, V2 would be met); no other verdict flips when any single log is dropped. Why the help is on those logs is not established (section 3, point 6).
6. **Equal cost (post hoc; cells run after the review of the first draft).** The audit's n/2 people could instead be random reporters. With 100 reporters and an audit of 50, heavy volunteers with audit-chosen weights score 0.626: far above the 50 audit people alone as random reporters (0.568; +0.0582, +0.0475 to +0.0684), above 100 random reporters (0.621; +0.0053, V7's comparison, which does not count the audit's people), but **below 150 random reporters (0.649): −0.0203 (−0.0276 to −0.0138), in none of 23 logs positive**. Most of that gap is the audit not entering any list: random reporters with the same audit also lose to 150 random reporters (−0.0275). Light volunteers score 0.601, below even 100 random reporters. Interpolated from the table (not a run), with an audit of 50 that only scores lists, 100 heavy volunteers beat random reporters at equal total cost at most if a volunteer costs less than about 0.6 of a random reporter (an upper bound; light volunteers about 0.3), assuming equal cost per head for audit people and random reporters.
7. **Size and scope.** The gains are about 0.012 NDCG at 100–300 people and 60–66% of the in-sample development gains (section 3, point 2). Simulated, activity-driven volunteers; the weight family is close to exactly right for them (a best case, section 5, point 4); the audit's own cost is not counted in V4–V10. Nothing here is a claim about real volunteers, taste differences or privacy.

## 1. What was done

1. **Why this test.** In doc 16 volunteers who send all their rows biased the list (S2): same-week heavy volunteers lost 0.0094 against random people, light volunteers 0.0236 (best fixed list); the selector kept working but the bias cost more than the selector could lose, and an audit sample of random people was not shown to help (S4). The classical remedy is to weight each reporter by the inverse of the chance of reporting (Hansen–Hurwitz, Horvitz–Thompson, or the self-normalised Hájek form); the server does not know the chance, but an unbiased random sample can tell which weights predict the next round best (Schnabel et al. 2016 use a small random sample to estimate propensities; doc 90, section 6, item 5). The lists already use per-round normalised weighted counts, so the Hájek form is what is tested.
2. **The reporters** (all send every row they have in the round): **random** people (std; the stored cell of doc 17), **heavy volunteers** (A+; the weight of being picked is proportional to the person's rows in the round) and **light volunteers** (A−; proportional to 1 / rows). The draw is Efraimidis–Spirakis successive sampling without replacement.
3. **The weights.** Each reporter's rows are weighted by rows^(−β), β in {−1, −0.5, 0, 0.5, 1}; β = 0 is the plain count of docs 14–16. The reporters picked do not depend on β, so all β see the same people (paired).
4. **The rules.** `rep0`: the selector of docs 14–15 on the reporters' self-score, β = 0 (the uncorrected baseline). `aud0`: the same selector on the audit's self-score (doc 16's audit selector). `audw`: **the proposal**, the same selector over all (β, memory) arms, scored on the audit's self-score (the audit sample is max(10, n/2) random people per round and never enters a list). Hindsight references: `best0` (best fixed memory at β = 0) and `bestw` (best fixed arm over all β), chosen with the truth. `ref`: `rep0` under random people.
5. **The cells.** Window w3 (the third window of doc 17), 24 logs (18 Amazon), 7-day rounds, 30, 100 and 300 people: 65 cells (24, 24 and 17 logs; 300 people exist for 17 logs only). The hypotheses use the log-level mean over a log's cells at 100 and 300 people (41 cells); 30 people is post hoc. The cells are built from the stored cells of doc 17: the random arms replicate them exactly (65 of 65, bit for bit).
6. **Development (in-sample, window w1).** 51 cells (27 logs at 100 people, 24 at 300) chose the plan. The in-sample gains were +0.0195 and +0.0236 (hindsight, heavy), +0.0193 and +0.0232 (audit-weighted against uncorrected, heavy), +0.0020 and +0.0025 (hindsight, light). These numbers are optimistic and are not results.
7. **Pre-registration.** Thresholds are about half of the development gains (a third for V10); the nulls and non-inferiority margins are 0.005. A same-provider design critic asked for V10 (to separate the weights from the audit), a V7 wording that claims no more than "not worse than random people with the same number of reporters", the Hájek remark and a best-case caveat. A code reviewer found no blocking defect. A prober ran the analysis on six synthetic worlds with known answers; the one mismatch it reported came from an arithmetic slip in its own planted numbers (its planted light-volunteer value could not reach the contrast it expected), not from the code.
8. **Run.** 65 cells on 2026-10-16, 5 workers, 32 minutes (22:30–23:02). No cell failed and none was dropped; `vol.jsonl.errors` is empty.
9. **Equal-cost cells (post hoc, after the review of the first draft of this document).** Random-people cells with the same code, window, draws, seed tag and selector, at 50 and 150 people (24 and 23 logs; 47 cells, about 90 seconds): the audit sizes of the 100- and 300-people cells, and 1.5 times 100. The days arms are those of the nearest registered size (50 → 30, 150 → 100). The selector function of the new script reproduces the stored random-people selector values of all 65 cells exactly, and 4 re-run cells are identical to the stored rows.

## 3. Interpretation

1. **Weights give a gain under heavy volunteers; the audit says which weights.** Heavy reporters send many rows and count more than the truth does (the truth counts each row once, but heavy and light people like different items). Shrinking each reporter's rows by rows^(−β) removes some of that, and an unbiased audit sample is enough to find a good β: its choice is within 0.0032 of the best fixed arm for every sampler (V9). The audit does not help without the weights (V10: the weights carry the gain; the audit's choice of memory alone adds +0.0001, −0.0006 to +0.0008, post hoc), which agrees with doc 16.
2. **Test gains are smaller than the development gains (by-size values are post hoc).** By people: V1 +0.0127 (100) and +0.0153 (300) against +0.0195 and +0.0236 in development; V4 +0.0115 and +0.0141 against +0.0193 and +0.0232; V10 +0.0113 and +0.0143 against +0.0187 and +0.0217 (the registry text gives +0.0145 for V10 at 100 people, which does not match the development table; section 5, point 2). The test values are 60–66% of the development values. The thresholds (about half of development) still held: V1 and V4 passed 0.01 by 34% and 24%, V10 passed 0.007 by 75%. V1 is a hindsight quantity in both stages, so its drop is not selection optimism: it reflects another window and other log sets (27 and 24 logs in development, 24 and 17 in the test).
3. **The heavy-volunteer loss is modest, and the gain about equals it where there is one (post hoc).** Uncorrected heavy volunteers lose to random people by 0.0063 at 100 people (−0.0006 to +0.0137, an interval that includes 0) and 0.0118 at 300 (+0.0059 to +0.0182); at 30 people they are ahead by 0.0098 without correction (loss −0.0098, −0.0205 to +0.0019, an interval that includes 0). The audit-weighted gain over the uncorrected selector (+0.0115, +0.0141) is larger than the pooled loss; the part beyond it (+0.0053, +0.0023) is V7's comparison, an identity (gain = loss + V7 margin), not a separate effect. Split by the observed sign of the loss (selection on the outcome, so indicative only; section 4, point 10): in the 15 logs where heavy volunteers lose, the gain about equals the loss (0.0174 against 0.0169); in the 9 logs where they were already ahead of random people (mean loss −0.0055) the audit-weighted selector ends 0.0096 above random people, and that is where most of V7's margin sits. That pattern fits "heavy reporters bring more rows" (about 3 times the rows per reporter at 100 people, 8.1 against 2.7; 2.1 times at 300) better than bias removal, but it is not tested separately (the control would be random people given the same number of rows). The V7 comparison is generous to the proposal: same number of reporters, uncounted audit, more rows per reporter.
4. **Random people: the audit costs a little.** When nobody is biased the audit-weighted selector picks a non-zero β in 31% (100 people) and 23% (300 people) of rounds and scores −0.0021 and −0.0008 against the uncorrected one (by-size values: post hoc); the extra arms and a noisy audit are the likely cause (not tested separately). At 30 people the cost is −0.0056, above the registered margin, so "safe" is shown from 100 people only. V3 is a null on a maximum over more arms and says nothing about harm; the safety claim rests on V6.
5. **Light volunteers are not brought up to random people.** V8 holds (−0.0177; −0.0202 at 100 people and −0.0138 at 300, post hoc), which is the size of the bias-only floor computed in doc 16 (section 4.3: about −0.02): weights did not close the gap. The audit-weighted selector recovers 26% (−12% to +47%) of the loss at 100 people and 40% (−6% to +63%) at 300 (post hoc; the intervals include 0).
6. **Why V2 and V5 missed: not established (post hoc).** V2 missed because of one log for its mean (ml-latest; section 4, point 6). Two candidate mechanisms, neither tested. The registered one is variance: correcting light volunteers needs large weights on the few heavy people who volunteer. It fits the by-exponent table (in hindsight the strong correcting exponent β = −1 is much worse than no weights, 0.552 against 0.599 at 100 people; the audit picks it in 6% of rounds at 100 people and 8% at 300). A second possibility is coverage: where light volunteers send about the same few rows each, no power of rows can reach the heavy people who are missing. The Amazon logs fit it (the loss of 0.0137 at 100 people stays; the audit-weighted selector recovers −15%, −22% to −10%, of it), but the help sits on only 3 of the 6 non-Amazon logs: ml-latest (+0.1056 hindsight, +0.1441 audit-weighted), goodreads-comics (+0.0289, +0.0369) and goodreads-children (+0.0190, +0.0230); together the six give +0.0259 and +0.0341, and 51% (13% to 64%) of the loss at 100 people. beeradvocate and ratebeer, whose people also have many rows (doc 18: 38% and 26% single-row person-weeks), show none (hindsight 0.0). No table here measures the spread of rows per person, so both mechanisms stay conjectures; six logs, one of them dominant, are too few to read a rate.
7. **V5's registered reason is not supported.** It expected the gain to come mostly from the audit choosing the memory. Post hoc, for light volunteers the audit's choice of memory gives +0.0019 (−0.0003 to +0.0058) and the weights +0.0057 (−0.0006 to +0.0159) of the +0.0076; both are small and uncertain.
8. **Equal cost: volunteers plus an audit lose to random reporters unless volunteers are cheap (post hoc).** At 100 reporters and an audit of 50 the heavy-volunteer arm scores 0.626 and 150 random reporters 0.649 (−0.0203, in none of 23 logs positive); light volunteers 0.601. Most of the gap is the audit that never enters a list: random reporters with the same audit also lose to 150 random reporters (−0.0275, −0.0332 to −0.0217), so heavy volunteers with an audit are about 0.007 better than random reporters with one. Not counting the audit's people, heavy volunteers are 0.005 ahead of 100 random reporters (V7's comparison; +0.0039 pooled). Break-even, interpolated from the table (not a run): the heavy arm's value equals that of about 109 random reporters, so with an audit of 50 that only scores lists, volunteers beat random reporters at equal total cost at most if a volunteer costs less than about 0.6 of a random reporter (about 0.3 for the light arm). The curve of value against reporters is concave, so linear interpolation overstates; a quadratic fit through the three points gives about 0.57 and 0.28. The cost model is implicit: cost per head, an audit person costs the same as a random reporter, a volunteer's cost does not depend on the rows sent. A pooled list (audit plus reporters) was not tested, and no break-even was estimated at 300 people.
9. **What to take from it (post hoc reading).** In this simulation, where the chance of reporting is a power of the rows (a best case), a random audit plus weights rows^(−β) with β picked on the audit gave a gain of about 0.012 NDCG over the uncorrected selector at 100–300 people when heavy people volunteer, and cost 0.0015 on the pooled mean when nobody was biased (0.0021 at 100 people, 0.0008 at 300, 0.0056 at 30). Whether this beats spending the same people as random reporters depends on their cost (point 8); at equal head count it did not. When light people volunteer, fix who is asked (doc 16); weights helped on three non-Amazon logs, one of them dominant, and why is not established (point 6).

## 5. Limits and disclosures

1. **Two smoke cells were run on test cells before the freeze** (amazon-video-games, window w3, 7-day rounds, 30 and 100 people), their values were seen (100 people: reporter selector under random people 0.4270; heavy volunteers 0.4291 uncorrected and 0.4353 audit-weighted; light volunteers 0.4145 and 0.4135), and the 100-people cell is one of the 41 that carry hypotheses. That log carries about 1/24 of the log-level weight and its audit-weighted gain (+0.0062) is below V4's threshold. The final rows of both cells equal the smoke cells bit for bit.
2. **The thresholds were set from the development numbers**, and the comparison cells (`ref`, the stored cells of doc 17) were seen before, so V7 and V8 are not blind on that side. Development on w1 is a hold-out in time, not in logs. There is no multiplicity correction across the 10 hypotheses (every one is reported). **A slip in the frozen registry text, found by the number audit after the run:** V10's prediction string gives +0.0145 as the development value at 100 people; the development table (`v_dev_grid.csv`) gives +0.0187 (audit-weighted 0.60651 minus audit selector 0.58781; +0.0217 at 300 people matches). The file is hash-frozen and was not edited, this is the erratum; every other development number in the registry (`dev_numbers`, used by V1–V8) was recomputed from the table and matches; the threshold 0.007 is about a third of the correct values (0.0187 and 0.0217), no verdict depends on it, and the test-to-development ratios in section 3, point 2 use the table values.
3. **Same 24 logs and window as doc 17** (18 Amazon, 6 other); the log-level intervals treat logs as independent and overstate independence. 300 people exist for 17 logs only (the 7 without are amazon-arts-crafts-and-sewing, amazon-cds-and-vinyl, amazon-video-games, beeradvocate, goodreads-comics, goodreads-poetry, ratebeer), so the log-level mean mixes sizes, as registered. n = 1,000 was not run (6 logs); rounds other than 7 days were not run.
4. **A best case for the weights.** The sampling weight of a volunteer is an exact power of the person's rows, which the server observes, so the weight family is close to correctly specified (the successive-sampling draw makes the chance of being included only approximately proportional to the weight, and at 300 people the chances saturate: median 3,650 active people per week, so the 0.5 step in β is coarse). A real server would have to estimate the chance (for example from the audit's distribution of rows per person); that is not tested.
5. **The audit sample is assumed unbiased and fully compliant** (random people who all answer); real random audits suffer non-response. Its cost (n/2 extra random people) is not counted in V4–V10; the equal-cost comparison of section 4, point 9 is post hoc, was prompted by a review of the first draft, and was run on the stored window with the same seed tag and selector but with the days arms of the nearest registered size (the 50 and 150 random reporters are a fresh draw, not the audit's people; the effect of the days-list choice is checked only at 50 people).
6. **Post hoc analyses were made after the V2 miss was seen** (group split, leave-one-log-out, recovery shares, equal cost), so they are explanations to be checked, not tests. Volunteer rules use activity only, never taste. A+ is an oracle rule (the weight uses the same week's rows). The truth is row-weighted popularity; person-weighted popularity was not tested and under it A− is unbiased. No claim about real volunteers.
7. **The Hansen–Hurwitz and Horvitz–Thompson "unbiased" property** is a statement about expected item counts up to scale, not about the top-100 list or NDCG; the self-normalised form used here is slightly biased by construction. The weight rows^(−β) is exact for a small sampling fraction when the chance of reporting is proportional to rows^β; with larger fractions the right exponent is smaller in size, which is why β is chosen on data. The weights are a diagnostic of bias, not a privacy mechanism; no noise is added.
8. **`rows_per_round` of a weighted arm adds up weights, not rows** and is not quoted as rows; the rows per reporter in section 4, point 4 come from the unweighted arms.
9. **Reviews are same-provider subagents, not an outside audit.** Before the freeze: a design critic, a code reviewer and a prober on six synthetic worlds with known answers. After the run: an Opus reviewer re-implemented every hypothesis from the raw rows without importing the project code and matched every verdict, mean and interval end (difference 0.0; 5·10⁻⁷ against the CSV rounding), the freeze (all 14 code and config hashes), all 65 std replications and the absence of any leak of the truth or of the audit into a list; it also found that V2 rests on one log for its mean (now in the summary). An Opus critic reviewed the first draft of this document (six findings: the count of met claims, the reading for light volunteers, the practice reading and the missing equal-cost test, the V7 wording and the rows-per-reporter ratio, unlabelled post hoc items, missing limits) and a second Opus critic the rewrite (six findings: the coverage mechanism stated as fact, the implicit cost model and a missing comparator row in the equal-cost analysis, the interpolation label, the heavy-volunteer reading, the count and decision wording, unlabelled post hoc items); every finding is applied in this version. An Opus reviewer checked the equal-cost script against programme G's cell rule (arms, window length, draws, seed tag, eligibility: 47 cells, 24 at 50 and 23 at 150 people), recomputed the equal-cost tables without importing project code (difference 5·10⁻⁷) and the interpolation arithmetic, and found no defect (two wording points, applied). Number audits: a Luna-tier auditor compared sections 4–7 with the tables (no mismatch); its audit of sections 0–3 stalled twice (provider error), so two Sonnet-tier scouts repeated it and found the registry slip above, three wording or rounding points (the V9 gap, the cost of a wrong exponent, a 75% margin) and no other mismatch; all are corrected. The post hoc scripts were run twice with byte-identical tables; the main analysis was re-run byte-identically.
10. **A governor with higher limits.** The run used 5 workers under the relaxed fan and temperature limits of `configs/ssp_quiet.json` (doc 18, section 5, point 8, and doc 90, section 8, item 6). It changes only when workers run, not any value.
11. **No new material.** No download, install or execution beyond the repository's own code (doc 90); the ledger check passed after the run.

## 6. What next

1. **Estimate the chance of reporting instead of picking an exponent** (π-estimated weights from the audit's distribution of rows per person, or the exponent from the distribution of rows per reporter) — the step from a best case to something a server could do.
2. **A pre-registered equal-cost test with explicit costs**: volunteers at a fraction of the price of a random reporter, an audit sized for the gain, and a pooled list (audit plus reporters) as a competitor.
3. **Light volunteers**: trimmed or smoothed weights (Epasto et al. 2020 style) against the variance problem; or a different lever, asking different people (doc 16). A test on logs where people differ in their number of rows, with more logs of that kind.
4. **Volunteers who differ in taste**, person-weighted popularity, and the combination with privacy (doc 18's unit-length reports are already a weight; both change the same counts).
